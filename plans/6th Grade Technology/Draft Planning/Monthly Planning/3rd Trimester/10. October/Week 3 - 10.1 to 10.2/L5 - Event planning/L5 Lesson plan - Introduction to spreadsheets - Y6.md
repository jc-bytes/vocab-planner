# Lesson 5: Event planning

Enjoyed teaching these lessons? Found a mistake? Share feedback at the-cc.io/feedback.

## Introduction

Learners will plan and calculate the cost of an event using a spreadsheet. They will use a predefined list to choose what they would like to include in their event, and use their spreadsheet to answer questions on the data they have selected. Learners will be reminded of the importance of organising data and will then create a spreadsheet using formulas to work out costs for their event.

## Learning objectives

To create a spreadsheet to plan an event

- I can use a spreadsheet to answer questions

- I can explain why data should be organised

- I can apply a formula to calculate the data I need to answer questions

## Key vocabulary

Propose, question, data set, data, organised, formula

## Preparation

Subject knowledge:

It would be beneficial to have an understanding of Google Sheets, including copying and pasting cells, entering data, creating formulas, using functions such as SUM, and duplicating formulas across multiple cells. These skills have been covered in previous lessons during this unit.

Learners have been provided with the mathematical calculations they need to complete the activities in this lesson in the ‘Data calculations’ handout. It is important that learners are given the opportunity to demonstrate their ability to use the computational skills required, regardless of their mathematical ability.

You will need:

- Slides

- A1 handout - Event planning

- A1 handout - Data calculations

- A1 resource - Party spreadsheet

- A3 resource - completed party spreadsheet example

- Homework sheet (optional)

## Assessment opportunities

Activity 1: Assess the learners’ ability to make sensible choices about their event and explain why data should be organised.

Activity 2: Assess the learners’ ability to create an organised spreadsheet to plan their event and use formulas to calculate totals.

Activity 3: Assess the learners’ ability to summarise data collected to determine if they have answered the given question.

Explain that they will need to enter the data they would like to include and will use the final spreadsheet to answer the question: “What is the total party cost for your event?”.<br>Note: Ensure the learners are aware that this is a hypothetical event and that it will not be taking place. It would be helpful to tell the learners how many pupils are in the class. |

| Activity 1<br>(Slides 4–7)<br>10 minutes | Entering and organising data<br>Tell the learners that they will have the opportunity to choose from the options shown on slide 4.<br>Show slide 5. Share the ‘Event planning’ handout with the learners. Tell them that they are going to choose which options they would like for their party. Point out the different sections. Inform the learners that they should pick one venue and then select what they would like from the other options.<br>Note: Teachers could change food items to more appropriate choices for their class if desired.<br>Bring the learners’ attention to the fact that p.p. means per person. Inform them that if something doesn’t say per person (p.p), the number needs to be divided by the amount of children in the class to work out the per person cost.<br>Tell the learners that they should first calculate the size of their budget. Show slide 6. Discuss that the budget is £15 per person, so they would need to multiply this by how many people are in the class/going to the party. For example, if there were ten people in the class, their total budget would be £150.<br>Show slide 7. Explain that the spreadsheet they will be using already includes data headings. Ask the learners, “Why are data headings important?” (answer: they enable us to keep the data organised). Click and ask the learners to think, pair, share, “Why should data be organised?” (answer: it makes it easier to read, understand, and perform calculations using the data).<br>Open the ‘Party’ spreadsheet and highlight the cell for the attendees. Explain that they can use the budget per person information already provided to work out the total budget.<br>Show the learners Tab 2 of the spreadsheet and explain that this tab contains all of the choices for their event. They can copy the information across or type these themselves, using the ‘Event planning’ handout. Model copying across an item from the list. The learners are not adding any quantity or subtotal information in this activity.<br>Note: Tab 2 includes spaces for visual purposes. This should not be replicated on the learners’ spreadsheets. This tab is included to ensure the learners have time to complete the rest of the lesson.<br>Show the learners how to access a copy of the ‘Party’ spreadsheet for this activity and provide them with the ‘Data calculations’ handout. Allow the learners time to make their choices and add them to the spreadsheet. |
| Activity 2<br>(Slides 8–11)<br>10 mins | Calculating data<br>Show slide 8. Tell the learners that they are now going to use the data in their spreadsheet to calculate the costs for their event.<br>Explain that there are two columns they need to think about. They will need to think about the quantity of each of their items and then use this to calculate the subtotals.<br>Show slide 9. Tell the learners that as they have included the number of attendees in cell G2, they can use this information to support their calculations. Think, pair, share, “What would happen if I entered this formula in the cell?” (answer: the cell would show the value in cell G2 (30) multiplied by one (30)).<br>Show slide 10. Explain that because multiplying by one doesn’t change the value, you can just write = G2 to duplicate the attendees.<br>Show slide 11. Open the ‘Party’ spreadsheet and model adding the formula =G2 in the quantity column for one of your food items. Ask the learners, “What you would need to write if you wanted each attendee to have two items each?” (answer: = G2 * 2 ). Explain that because you have used a cell reference, this will automatically update if you need to increase/decrease the number of attendees.<br>Remind the learners that if the item is not costed per person (p.p.), they do not need to use a formula. They can just write this cost into the cell directly.<br>Explain that you can now work out the subtotal column for this item. Think, pair, share, “What do you need to do to work out the subtotal?” (answer: multiply the cost of the item and the quantity). Remind learners to use their knowledge of duplicating formulas to help them fill out their sheet.<br>Ask learners to return to their spreadsheets and begin calculating the data for the quantity and subtotal columns. Remind the learners to use their ‘Data calculations’ handout to support them. |
| Activity 3<br>(Slides 12–14)<br>15 mins | The final figures<br>Bring the learners back together and show slide 12 to explain that they now need to calculate their final figures. In order to work out the total cost for each spending type, they will need to add together all of the subtotals for each item that belongs to that spending type.<br>Once all of the cells have been completed, the learners will be able to calculate the budget left over, total party cost, and party cost per person. They need to add their formulas into the cells underneath overall party costs.<br>Show slide 13. Ask the learners to return to their spreadsheets. Encourage them to use their ‘Data calculations’ handout to complete the missing calculations. Remind learners to ensure all of their spreadsheet is formatted in the correct way. For example, the cost column is formatted for currency.<br>Once the learners have had time to complete their spreadsheets, share the question on slide 14. Think, pair, share, “Can you answer the question using the data on your spreadsheet?”. Ask the learners to spend some time thinking and discussing what other information they could tell you, using the data on their completed spreadsheets. Take some suggestions from the learners. |
| Plenary<br>(Slides 15–16)<br>5 mins | Creating graphs<br>Show slide 15. Tell the learners that spreadsheets can be used to create graphs. Think, pair, share, “What does this pie chart tell you?”. Answers may include:<br>The majority of the budget is left over — this is shown by the large section of orange on the pie chart<br>The smallest cost was drinks — the purple section of the pie chart is the smallest<br>Show slide 16. Ask the learners, “What would happen to the pie chart if we spent more money on essentials and decorations?” (green segment). Click through the animation. Discuss that the essentials and decorations section (green segment) of the pie chart would be bigger as more money has been spent in this area.<br>Tell the learners that any changes to the cells holding pie chart data will be reflected on the chart if they have used the cell formulas correctly. |

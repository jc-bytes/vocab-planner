# Lesson 6: Improving and sharing

Enjoyed teaching these lessons? Found a mistake? Share feedback at the-cc.io/feedback.

## Introduction

In this lesson, learners build on the project that they created in Lesson 5. They consider how they could improve their own projects and make small changes to achieve this. Learners then have the opportunity to add a variable independently. Finally, learners evaluate each other’s projects; they identify features that they liked and features that could be improved.

## Learning objectives

To evaluate my project

- I can identify ways that my game could be improved

- I can use variables to extend my game

- I can share my game with others

## Key vocabulary

Improve, evaluate, share

## Preparation

Subject knowledge:

- This lesson gives learners the opportunity to improve their projects and apply what they have learnt from throughout the unit. No new blocks or concepts are introduced. However, it is useful to have a good all-round knowledge of Scratch for this lesson, as learners may choose a variety of different ways to improve their project.

You will need:

- L6 slides

- Devices that are capable of running Scratch 3

- A2 Scratch project – Banana overload (the-cc.io/bananas)

- A3 Activity sheet – Sharing and evaluating

## Assessment opportunities

Activity 1: You can assess whether the learners can identify how their own projects can be improved.

Activity 2: You can assess whether the learners can use a new variable to extend their games.

Activity 3: You can assess whether the learners can share a project in Scratch and constructively evaluate another project.

## Outline plan

Please note that the slide deck labels the activities in the top right-hand corner to help you navigate the lesson.

*Timings are rough guides

| Introduction<br>(Slide 3)<br>5 mins | Are all games the same?<br>Display slide 3. Explain that Scratch is an open source platform where people can share their projects for others to use and adapt. As a result, there are lots of versions of fruit catcher games. Some are based on the same template project used in this unit, while others are completely different.<br>To show a selection of the projects to the whole class, click on the images on the slide (these are hyperlinked to the projects themselves).<br>Ask the learners to think, pair, share some of the similarities and differences between these projects and their own work. Encourage the learners to think about how they could use some of the ideas from these projects to improve their project. |
| --- | --- |
| Activity 1<br>(Slide 4)<br>10 mins | Improve your project<br>Display slide 4. Give the learners an opportunity to think about what they would change in their projects. Explain that they can make any improvements they like, but the fundamental objective of the game must remain the same — to catch falling objects.<br>Encourage the learners to use ideas from projects that were explored in the introduction activity. These might include:<br>Changing the speed of the different falling sprites in the game to increase the game’s difficulty<br>Altering the sprites or background<br>Changing the speed of the Bowl sprite<br>Adding a sprite that reduces the score |
| Activity 2 (Slides 5–6)<br>15 mins | Add a ‘lives’ variable<br>Display slide 5. Share the ‘Banana overload’ Scratch project and emphasise the ‘missed’ variable in this game. Explain to the learners that they will adapt this variable to complete the task of creating a ‘lives’ variable. Explain that the code snippet on the slide is the code for this variable.<br>Share slide 6. Show the learners the process that they should go through (the four levels of design), to add the new variable. For this activity, there is no template for the algorithm design; learners should choose how they would like to represent the algorithm, but they can use the code on the slide to help them.<br>Give the learners time to add the ‘lives’ variable to their programs. |
| Activity 3<br>(Slides 7–8)<br>10 mins | Share your project<br>Display side 7. Ask the learners to save their project, and to click on Share, so that other learners can access it.<br>Now that they have evaluated how they can improve their own project, in this activity the learners evaluate another learner’s project. Hand out the activity sheet and ask the learners to fill in their own name and the nine-digit number at the end of their project’s URL. Emphasise that they need to copy the number carefully.<br>Once all the learners have written down their name and the URL, redistribute the activity sheets around the classroom.<br>Share slide 8. Ask the learners to access the project on the sheet that they have been given and complete the evaluation table.<br>Emphasise that comments should be positive and constructive. If necessary, for the ‘How could it be even better?’ section, prompt learners to use sentence starters, such as “It would be even better if…”. Once the learners have completed the table, ask them to return the activity sheet to the learner that it belongs to. |
| Plenary<br>(slides 9)<br>5 mins | Reflection<br>Share slide 9. Ask the learners to read the comments they have been given by their peers and to reflect on the ‘How could it be even better?’ comments. Learners won’t have the opportunity to change their projects in this unit of work, but encourage them to consider how they could improve further projects using the constructive feedback they were given. |
| Review<br>(Slides 10–11)<br>5 mins | This time<br>Review the ‘Assessment’ and ‘Summary’ slides. |

# Lesson 4: Designing a game

Enjoyed teaching these lessons? Found a mistake? Share feedback at the-cc.io/feedback.

## Introduction

In this lesson, learners work at the ‘design’ level of abstraction, where they create their artwork and algorithms. Learners first design the sprites and backgrounds for their project, then they design their algorithms to create their program flow.

## Learning objectives

To design a project that builds on a given example

- I can choose the artwork for my project

- I can create algorithms for my project

- I can explain my design choices

## Key vocabulary

Design, algorithm, code

## Preparation

Subject knowledge:

- You will need to be familiar with the four levels of design — known as ‘levels of abstraction’. In this lesson, learners move between the ‘design’ and ‘running the code’ levels of abstraction. You can find more information about levels of abstraction in the unit overview.

- This lesson and Lesson 5 also involve Use-Modify-Create as a pedagogy to teach programming. In this lesson, learners use elements of an existing project and consider how they could modify it. In Lesson 5, they will create their own project.

- You will need a clear understanding of the definition of the term ‘algorithm’. This is defined in the outline plan below.

You will need:

- L4 slides

- Devices that are capable of running Scratch 3

- A0 Scratch project – Fruit catcher intro (the-cc.io/introfruit)

- A1 Activity sheet – Project design

- A1 Scratch project – Fruit catcher star (the-cc.io/fruitcatcher)

## Assessment opportunities

Activity 1: Assess whether learners can choose appropriate artwork for their project.

Activity 2: Assess whether learners can make good design choices and adapt an existing example of an algorithm to represent those design choices.

Activity 3: Assess whether learners can understand the concept of program flow and apply it to their own algorithms.

| Introduction<br>(Slide 3)<br>10 mins | Analyse a game<br>Display slide 3. Direct learners towards the ‘Fruit catcher remix’ Scratch project on the slide. Leave the questions visible on the slide, and ask learners to try the project (a straightforward catching game). Once they have had a short amount of time to explore the game, ask them to think, pair, share their answers to the questions on the slide.<br>Discuss learners’ responses. They should identify the following:<br>There is a Bowl sprite that can be moved left and right using the arrow keys on the keyboard to catch the falling sprites.<br>There are eight sprites. (Learners may notice that there are four versions of the Apple sprite.)<br>Some of the sprites fall down the screen. The Bowl sprite can be moved left and right. The Screen bottom sprite doesn’t move.<br>The falling sprites fall at different speeds.<br>There is a score in the game, which changes by different amounts when the falling sprites hit the bowl. |
| --- | --- |
| Activity 1<br>(Slides 4–6)<br>10 mins | Choose your artwork<br>Display slide 4. Share the learners’ task and explain that they will create a project that meets the task requirements over the next few lessons.<br>Display slide 5. Provide learners with access to the ‘Fruit catcher star’ Scratch project template and the activity sheet. Explain that they should use this project as the basis for their game.<br>Show slide 6. Explain to the learners that they need to choose two additional sprites to fall down the screen and a background for their project. For ease of design, encourage learners to choose sprites and a background that are already available in Scratch. They can use Scratch to find examples of these if necessary. Ask learners to record their choices on the activity sheet. Tell them to include the name of each sprite and the background. |
| Activity 2 (Slides 7–9)<br>15 mins | Design your algorithms<br>Note: In the previous activity, learners saw the project in action. In this activity, they work at the ‘design’ level of abstraction, where they focus on creating their algorithms. This activity deliberately avoids the ‘code’ level.<br>Display slide 7 and remind learners that an algorithm is a precise sequence of instructions, or set of rules, for performing a task.<br>Display slide 8. Show the Scratch template project and then the example of an algorithm for the falling star on the slide. Emphasise that this algorithm includes a diagram and a written description.<br>Display slide 9. Direct the learners back to their activity sheet and ask them to design algorithms for the two sprites that they have added. Show learners the suggestions on the slide to help them get started. |
| Activity 3<br>(Slide 10)<br>5 mins | Explain your design<br>Display slide 10. Ask the learners to explain their design to a partner. This should be a quick explanation to show the choices each learner has made. The learners should be able to answer all of the questions on the slide. |
| Plenary<br>(Slide 11)<br>5 mins | Other algorithms<br>Display slide 11. Explain to the learners that they have focused on the algorithms for a particular part of the project: the sprites that move down the screen. Ask learners to think about which other algorithms are already represented as code in this project.<br>Learners should identify that:<br>The Screen bottom sprite sets the score to zero at the beginning of each game<br>The Bowl sprite moves left and right when the left and right arrow keys on the keyboard are pressed |

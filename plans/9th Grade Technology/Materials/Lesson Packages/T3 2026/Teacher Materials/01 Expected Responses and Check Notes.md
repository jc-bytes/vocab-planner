# Grade 9 Technology, Trimester 3 expected responses and check notes

Teacher-only. Do not distribute with student practice.

## Practice Sheet A

Accept different problems when the data fits the purpose. A complete answer names a real problem, affected users, one recordable value or pattern, and a decision the evidence could support. Example structure: excessive cafeteria noise; students and staff; sound-level readings at fixed times; decide where or when noise controls are needed.

## Practice Sheet B

1. `24 × 12 = 288 pixels`.
2. `288 × 8 = 2,304 bits`.
3. RGB `255,255,0` is yellow because red and green are at maximum intensity and blue is zero.
4. `10 × 8 × 2 = 160 bits`.
5–6. Accept a specific setting change with both sides of the trade-off. For example, increasing sample rate can capture more detail but increases uncompressed size.

Watch for adding dimensions, omitting units, treating RGB as paint, or confusing samples per second with bits per sample.

## Practice Sheet C

Expected threat matches: phishing; brute-force attack; trojan or malware; DDoS; known software vulnerability. Protections may vary. They must be safe and act on the stated route or consequence. Useful examples include verifying through the official site and MFA, long unique passwords with login-rate limits, avoiding untrusted downloads and scanning, service filtering/rate limiting, and verified security updates.

Do not accept advice to reply to suspicious senders, test malware, attack back, share credentials, or disable protections.

## Practice Sheet D

- Weekly phishing: probability is supported by weekly attempts. MFA may reduce impact or successful access, but it does not erase the attempts.
- Offline reset laptop: data-theft impact is low because it stores no personal data and has little lasting information.
- Unbacked project folder: monthly deletion supports high probability. Impact depends on project importance and recovery difficulty; a tested backup is the direct protection.

Accept alternate quadrants when the student cites a relevant fact and explains the decision. The priority should follow the student's own ratings.

## Project habits sheets

Evidence must already exist. Good examples include a dated log entry, named file version, teacher-observed safe procedure, recorded feedback, assigned role completed, or corrected test result. A claim such as “I worked hard” needs a concrete action or record.

## Exit-response guide

The exit bank is diagnostic, not a hidden grade. Sort responses into: ready, needs one correction, or reteach. Begin the next class with one anonymous misconception. Do not reveal formal assessment answers before all groups complete the task.

## Collection

Accept the assigned saved file, exported module report, share link, typed test log, printed fallback, or live teacher-observed demonstration. Confirm that a file opens before marking evidence present. Do not request evidence from a separate recording device.

## Make-up decisions

Preserve the same objective and response count. Change scenario wording or numeric values when answer sharing is possible. For a missed live project presentation, use the project make-up conference with the final product, logbook, three test records, improvement explanation, and reflection.
